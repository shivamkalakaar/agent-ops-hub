How Shivam writes. He rewrote an agent draft into the post below and wants every post built this way. The agent's draft had the same facts; his version wins because it says the point first, explains each thing before leaning on it, and gives the reader something to do with it.

> AI coding benchmark scores can look much better than they really are.
>
> Alibaba and several Asian universities released a new coding benchmark for agents. It's built from real user change requests on existing web apps, and it focuses on the messy ones, where the user had to rephrase the request or roll back a change.
>
> The best model met about 70% of each task's requirements. But it completed the full task correctly only 26.4% of the time.
>
> So high requirement coverage doesn't mean the task got done. Most of the time the agent did part of the job and left the rest for a person to fix.
>
> Why a new benchmark? Older coding benchmarks are saturated. Models score above 99% on them once they're allowed to test and repair their own work.
>
> What surprised me more is the harness effect. Qwen3.8-27B scored 75% on larger overhauls in one harness and 56% in another. That's a nineteen-point gap, and the model itself didn't change. Even the model rankings depended on the harness.
>
> The authors recommend always reporting the model and the harness together, and evaluating them as a pair before you pick an agent.
>
> Next time you see a coding eval score, ask two questions. Is it requirement coverage or full task completion? And what harness produced it?
>
> #ai #coding

# The structure (every post, in this order)
1. The claim. One plain sentence that says the point of the whole post to any reader. No numbers, no names, no codes. It is a claim the rest of the post proves, not a teaser.
2. What happened. Who did what, and what is new or different about it, in one to two plain sentences. Explain the thing (what the benchmark measures, what the product does) before any result.
3. The key result. The one or two numbers that carry the claim, side by side so the contrast is obvious. A short "But" sentence is fine.
4. "So ..." What the result means in everyday terms: what actually happens to a person, a team or a budget. This is where the reader gets it.
5. Optional: the question a reader would ask next ("Why a new benchmark?", "Why now?"), asked in a few words and answered in one or two sentences.
6. Optional: a second finding from the same story that makes the claim stronger, introduced with his honest reaction ("What surprised me more is ..."). Give its number and spell out in plain words why it is surprising ("and the model itself didn't change").
7. Optional: what the source, the authors or a named person recommend or warn, attributed.
8. The ending: a practical check the reader can use next time they meet this (questions to ask, what to look for, what to compare), tied straight back to the claim in step 1. One to three short sentences.
Then exactly two hashtags on their own last line.

# What makes it work
- The point comes first, in plain words, and every paragraph after it proves or explains it. A reader who stops after line one still got the idea.
- Nothing is used before it is explained. A term the post needs (here "harness", "requirement coverage") is introduced in context, then used the same way every time. Never swap in a synonym for variety.
- Numbers are few and compared. Each one is followed by what it means.
- Paragraphs are one to three short sentences. Connecting words ("But", "So", "Why ...?", "Even") carry the reader from one step to the next.
- Attribution stays: who made the claim, who ran the test, who recommends what.
- His voice is in short honest reactions ("What surprised me more") and in the ending's advice, not in clever lines.

# Easy to read
His followers know AI well and are technical, and they still stop reading when a post is dense. Write for a smart engineer from a different part of AI, reading on a phone:
- Only the numbers that carry the idea, usually four to six. Plain ones (percentages, dollars, counts, "2.3 times") read fast; specs and units (bit widths, bytes, tokens a second per user) slow everyone down: explain them in plain words or leave them out.
- Say what a thing does, not what it's called. A spec or acronym goes in only if the idea needs it, and then with a few plain words on what it means. Never two in one sentence.
- One fact per sentence. Most sentences under 20 words; none over 30.
- If a reader would have to reread a sentence, it's wrong.

# Rules
- One claim. A second finding is welcome only if it backs the same claim; facts that don't serve it stay out.
- Name the company behind each model.
- Never invent first-hand experience: no people met, no "I get asked this". Quotes come from the sources, with who said them.
- 150-220 words. Exactly two hashtags, on the last line.
- No clever closer, aphorism or dry one-liner at the end: end on the reader's practical check.
- Never copy the example's phrases ("can look much better than they really are", "What surprised me more", "Next time you see", "ask two questions"); use the same moves in your own words, and vary the ending from post to post.
